# Micro-level evidence on individual online-shopper behavior, segmentation and UX/survey benchmarks (for validating TwinStore's simulated online shoppers)

Scope note: this file covers the micro level (visit types, paths, search, grid clicks, reviews, waiting, demographics, accessibility, abandonment reasons, exit-survey benchmarks, multi-session journeys). It does not cover raw public datasets, aggregate KPI benchmarks by industry, causal design-lever experiments, LLM-agent literature or Korea-specific data, which other researchers handle. "Secondary" marks figures I could only reach through press coverage, vendor blogs or search-engine extraction, not the primary document. Several primary pages (Pew, ACSI, ACM CACM, ResearchGate, PubMed) returned HTTP 403/503 to the fetcher; where that happened, it is noted.

## 1. Shopper visit typologies / missions, and the empirical distribution of page views and session length

### Takeaway
Clickstream research consistently finds that most visits do not end in a purchase. Per-visit conversion in early panel data was about 7–14% for big retailers, and most visits start in a browsing/exploratory state. Moe (2003) gives the canonical four-way typology of directed buying, search/deliberation, hedonic browsing and knowledge-building. Session depth is heavy-tailed: the median is about 5 pages, the mean is higher, and some sessions run to hundreds of pages. Huberman et al. (1998) model the number of pages visited per site as an inverse Gaussian.

### Cited Findings
**Visit typology (Moe 2003; Moe & Fader 2004)**
- Moe, W. W. (2003), "Buying, Searching, or Browsing: Differentiating Between Online Shoppers Using In-Store Navigational Clickstream," *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 13(1–2): 29–39, DOI 10.1207/S15327663JCP13-1&2_03. It develops and tests a typology of four store-visit strategies: directed buying, search/deliberation, hedonic browsing and knowledge-building. Session variables include visit depth (pages/products viewed), time on site, page types viewed and variety of categories browsed. — [Wiley abstract](https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1207/S15327663JCP13-1%262_03)
- Moe & Fader (2004) define the four types as follows. **Directed buyers** "have a specific product purchase in mind when entering the store and ... are unlikely to exit the store without a purchase." **Search/deliberation** visitors are also goal-directed but "only have a general product category in mind," and they may buy after a series of visits. **Hedonic browsers** "enter a retailer with no product or even product category in mind," and any purchase looks like an "impulse" occurrence. **Knowledge-building** visitors "have no intention of buying." The same text says Moe (2003) extends **Janiszewski's (1998)** dichotomy of offline shopping into exploratory vs. directed search. — [Moe & Fader 2004, Management Science 50(3): 326–335, PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/Moe-Fader-Conversion-Mgmt-Sci-2004.pdf)
- Moe & Fader (2004) data: a Media Metrix panel of about 10,000 households, Amazon.com, March 1 – October 31, 1998. The data have **4,379 visitors, 851 buyers (19.4% of visitors ever bought), 11,301 visits and 1,573 purchases**. That gives an **overall per-visit conversion of 13.9%**, **2.58 visits per visitor**, **1.85 purchases per buyer** and **0.36 purchases per visitor**. Conversion was 13.2% in months 1–4 and 14.7% in months 5–8. For "active" shoppers seen in both early and late months, conversion *fell* from 26.0% to 20.8%, a sign that aggregate trends can hide individual dynamics. The paper also says "typical conversion rates rarely exceed 5%" for e-commerce. — [Moe & Fader 2004 PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/Moe-Fader-Conversion-Mgmt-Sci-2004.pdf)
- Moe & Fader (2004) results: visits have positive, accumulating effects on purchase probability, but each later visit has a diminishing effect (k = 0.932 < 1). Purchasing thresholds rise after purchases. The full model includes a "hard-core never-buyer" segment of size 1 − π, and Table 3 estimates π = 0.790. **My reading of Table 3 is that about 21% of visitors are modeled as never-buyers.** A latent-segment logistic benchmark found two segments: 72% respond little to past purchases and 28% are very sensitive to them. — [Moe & Fader 2004 PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/Moe-Fader-Conversion-Mgmt-Sci-2004.pdf)
- A 2002 working-paper draft by Moe and co-authors ("A Bayesian Treed Model of Online Purchasing Behavior") reportedly states that only 1–2% of visits to a typical online retailer convert. This is secondary, via search snippet, and was not verified in the full text. **I could not find the cluster shares of the four visit types in Moe (2003)**; see Gaps. — [document listing](https://www.youscribe.loungeup.com/catalogue/documents/art-musique-et-cinema/a-bayesian-treed-model-of-online-purchasing-behavior-using-in--397499)

**Path analysis (Montgomery, Li, Srinivasan & Liechty 2004, Marketing Science 23(4))**
- Data: Comscore Media Metrix panel, **1,160 users visiting barnesandnoble.com in April 2002**, with **1,659 sessions**. A session ends after 20 minutes of no page viewing. The **average was 8.75 B&N pages viewed per session, with a median of 5 and a range of 2–239**. Fewer than 25% of users had more than two sessions. **114 of 1,659 sessions had a purchase, a 7% conversion rate.** Panelists averaged 46 years old (range 9–89), and slightly more than half were female. — [Montgomery et al. 2004 (Feb 2004 revision PDF)](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/alm3/papers/purchase%20conversion.pdf)
- Entry pages: users "tend to start either at an **information (60%), home (16%), or category page (16%)**." There is strong persistence in page type, with the diagonal transition probabilities between .43 and .69. The average time between viewings is 7.2 s, inflated downward by many one-second repeat viewings. 45% of pages showed price information and 83% showed promotion information. — [Montgomery et al. 2004 PDF](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/alm3/papers/purchase%20conversion.pdf)
- A two-state hidden Markov model fit best. **64% of users start in a browsing-oriented state.** Users stay in the browsing state for about 3 viewings and in the deliberation state for about 4. Purchases are more likely in the deliberation state. **After only six viewings, purchasers can be predicted with >40% accuracy, compared with a 7% baseline.** Simulated customization after the 5th viewing would raise conversion from about 7% to more than 9%. — [Montgomery et al. 2004 PDF](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/alm3/papers/purchase%20conversion.pdf)

**Within-site browsing (Bucklin & Sismeiro 2003, JMR 40(3))**
- The preliminary 2001 slide version uses server logs from a car-manufacturer site (with a vehicle configurator) for October 1999. It covers **169,910 visitors, 245,782 sessions and 1.46M page requests**, with **1.447 sessions per visitor, 5.95 pages per session and an average page-view duration of 102.7 s**. A random sample of 5,000 gave 6.12 pages per session and 117 s. Sessions break after 30 minutes of inactivity. — [Bucklin & Sismeiro, Choice Symposium 2001 slides](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/alm3/presentations/choicesymposium2001/bucklin.pdf)
- Model findings: the probability of requesting another page falls with cumulative pages in the visit (LN(CPAGE) = −0.711) and with repeat visits, which the authors read as "learning." **Server response time strongly lowers the probability of continuing (SRVRESP = −8.49) and also lowers page-view duration.** More bytes per page raise both stay probability and duration. A longer previous page view lowers the next request probability. — [Bucklin & Sismeiro slides](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/alm3/presentations/choicesymposium2001/bucklin.pdf)

**Goal-directed vs. experiential (Wolfinbarger & Gilly 2001; Rohm & Swaminathan 2004)**
- Wolfinbarger & Gilly, "Shopping Online for Freedom, Control, and Fun," *California Management Review* 43(2): 34–55 (2001). They report that "goal-oriented motives are more common among online shoppers than are experiential motives." Drivers are accessibility/convenience, selection, information availability and lack of unwanted sociality. Online shopping gives "a substantially increased sense of freedom and control." The method is qualitative: 5 offline and 4 online focus groups, with Harris Interactive. — [CMR](https://cmr-mig.berkeley.edu/2001/02/43-2-shopping-online-for-freedom-control-and-fun)
- Rohm & Swaminathan (2004), *Journal of Business Research* 57(7): 748–757. Based on online convenience, physical-store orientation, information use and variety seeking, they identify four types: **convenience shoppers, variety seekers, balanced buyers and store-oriented shoppers**. Cluster sizes were not found. — [LMU Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.lmu.edu/mbl_fac/21)

**Micro-moments (Think with Google, 2015)**
- "82% of smartphone users consult their phones while in a store deciding which product to buy," and 1 in 10 of them buy a different product than planned. This is Google-commissioned research, so it is secondary-grade. — [Think with Google](https://thinkwithgoogle.com/marketing-resources/micro-moments/how-micromoments-are-changing-rules)

**Heavy-tailed surfing depth (Huberman et al. 1998)**
- Huberman, Pirolli, Pitkow & Lukose, "Strong regularities in World Wide Web surfing," *Science* 280(5360): 95–97 (1998), DOI 10.1126/science.280.5360.95. A random-walk model gives a **two-parameter inverse Gaussian distribution for the number of pages (links) a user visits within a site**, with mean μ and variance μ³/λ. The tail is very long, decaying roughly as L^−3/2, so typical users differ from the average user. The primary article was not reachable. Descriptions come from a Xerox patent and later reviews (secondary). — [US Patent 6115718 (Xerox PARC) text](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6115718); [CACM 2006, "Mobile web surfing is the same as web surfing"](https://cacmb4.acm.org/magazines/2006/3/5985-mobile-web-surfing-is-the-same-as-web-surfing)
- Halvey, Keane & Smyth (CACM 2006) report that the same "law of surfing" holds on mobile WAP portals. In that summary, most users select only 2–3 links per session (secondary). — [CACM 2006](https://cacmb4.acm.org/magazines/2006/3/5985-mobile-web-surfing-is-the-same-as-web-surfing)

### Inferences
- TwinStore's default mission mix (purchase 25 / browse 35 / compare 15 / deal_hunt 10 / gift 7 / replenish 8) maps loosely onto Moe's types. "purchase" and "replenish" correspond to directed buying (33% combined). "compare", "gift" and "deal_hunt" correspond to search/deliberation (32%). "browse" covers hedonic browsing and knowledge-building (35%). There is no published Moe cluster share to test this against directly. A defensible check is that about 64% of B&N sessions *start* in a browsing state (Montgomery et al. 2004), and that Moe & Fader estimate about 21% of Amazon visitors to be never-buyers.
- If TwinStore's per-visit purchase rate for a large, well-known retailer lands in the single digits to low teens, that is consistent with the 1998–2002 panel numbers: Amazon 13.9%, B&N 7%. Modern single-store conversion benchmarks are lower and are covered elsewhere.
- For page-count validation, a simulated distribution of pages per session should be right-skewed, with the median about 5–6 and the mean above the median (8.75 vs. 5 at B&N). It should fit an inverse Gaussian or log-normal better than a normal. Reporting the median, the mean and a tail statistic is more convincing than the mean alone.
- Bucklin & Sismeiro's 103–117 s average page-view duration and Montgomery's 7.2 s inter-viewing time measure different things (dwell vs. clicks including back-button repeats). The paper should state which TwinStore's dwell model corresponds to.

### Gaps
- The share of sessions in each of Moe's four clusters (2003) could not be retrieved; the full text is paywalled. Do not cite any specific split without checking the article.
- I found no McKinsey/Google primary figure for "share of visits that are browsing vs. buying."
- Rohm & Swaminathan cluster sizes were not found.
- The exact fitted inverse-Gaussian parameters from Huberman et al. (1998) were not retrieved.

## 2. Site search behavior

### Takeaway
On-site product queries are short, mostly 1–2 words. Baymard's taxonomy (exact, product type, feature, use case/thematic, abbreviation/symbol, compatibility, symptom, non-product) is the reference for query *types*. Many sites handle the less obvious types poorly, and about 4–15% of searches return zero results. Roughly half of users prefer search over navigation, though the classic "search-dominant users" claim is old and contested.

### Cited Findings
- **Baymard query types (2024 article, updated Apr 2026), with the share of benchmarked sites that have issues for each type:** Exact (full title/model no.) 12%; Product Type ("sandals") 20%; Feature (attributes, brand, price, e.g., "size 8 sneakers") 39%; Use Case ("party dress", "gaming chair"; formerly "thematic") 43%; Abbreviation & Symbol ("13in laptop sleeve") 54%; Compatibility ("Dell laptop adapter") 44%; Symptom ("stained rug", "dry cough") 37%; Non-Product ("return policy", "cancel my order") 66%. Basis: 10,000+ usability scores across 170+ benchmarked sites and apps. 56% of sites fail to adequately support users' search needs. — [Baymard, E-commerce Search Query Types](https://baymard.com/blog/ecommerce-search-query-types)
- Baymard: "roughly half of participants prefer search as their product-finding strategy," and the other half prefer main navigation. In Baymard's Accounts & Self-Service testing, **34% of participants used the search field for non-product content**. — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/ecommerce-search-query-types)
- Baymard (2014 original; secondary summaries) reported that only 34% of 50 benchmarked sites supported thematic/feature/symptom query types. Its 2019 study found that 60% of sites could not return all results for product-type synonyms. — [Baymard Search Report & Benchmark](https://baymard.com/blog/ecommerce-search-report-and-benchmark) (figures via secondary search summary)
- **Query length (SLI Systems, 1.5 billion site-search queries, 2013–Q1 2017):** 56% of queries were one word, 26% two words and 11% three words. One-word queries rose from 45% (2013), and queries of three or more words fell from 24% to 18%. This is secondary, via MarketingCharts. — [MarketingCharts](https://www.marketingcharts.com/industries/retail-and-e-commerce-76709)
- Amazon-wide query sample (39.6M queries, Dejan SEO): very-high-volume queries average about 2.6 words / 16 characters, and very-low-volume ones about 3.9 words / 23 characters. The author notes that the distributions overlap heavily. — [dejan.ai](https://dejan.ai/blog/query-length-vs-volume/)
- **Zero-result rate (Luigi's Box, 4,000 online stores, 15 verticals):** an average of **6.3%** of searches return no results, ranging from 9.1% (Pets) and 8.5% (Electronics) to 4.3% (Fashion) and 4.2% (Luxury). **About one-third of customers leave immediately after a no-result search**, from 28% (Pets) to 40% (Books & Games, Marketplaces). This is a vendor source, and the data year is not stated. — [Luigi's Box](https://www.luigisbox.com/blog/searches-without-results-data/)
- Other vendor estimates put zero results at 10–15% of searches. Definitions differ (unique searches vs. results pageviews). — [Hello Retail 2026](https://helloretail.com/en/blog/2026-02-24-ecommerce-search-statistics/)
- **Search vs. navigation, classic claim:** Nielsen's 1997 Alertbox said "slightly more than half of all users are search-dominant, about a fifth are link-dominant, and the rest exhibit mixed behavior" (secondary quote). Jared Spool's UIE study of 30 users on 121 shopping tasks found that **no user always used search**, 18% always used categories and the rest were mixed. On 21% of sites, every user went to search immediately, so search use depends on the site. — [UIE / Center Centre, "Are There Users Who Always Search?"](https://articles.centercentre.com/?p=15); [Zope list quoting Nielsen 1997](https://mail.zope.org/pipermail/zope-cmf/2001-July/008414.html)
- NN/g senior study: seniors used search engines 51% more than younger users to complete tasks. — [NN/g 2013](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-seniors-improvements/)

### Inferences
- TwinStore's query generator can be checked against the length distribution: most queries should be 1–2 words, with a minority of 3+. It can also be checked against type coverage: a realistic mix includes exact, product-type, feature and use-case queries plus some non-product queries ("return policy"). A persona that types long natural-language sentences would be unrealistic for most shoppers.
- When a simulated store returns zero results, about one-third exiting immediately is a usable micro-benchmark for the "leave" decision after a failed search.
- A roughly 50/50 split between search-first and navigate-first visitors among those with a product in mind is defensible. It should vary by site and catalog type rather than being a fixed persona trait (Spool).

### Gaps
- I found no primary, recent (2023–2026) peer-reviewed measure of average e-commerce query length in characters, or of the share of searches that are refined or reformulated. The SLI figures are from 2017 and come via a secondary source.
- Forrester's often-quoted "43% go directly to search" figure was not verified.

## 3. Product listing/grid behavior and position bias

### Takeaway
Attention and clicks concentrate at the top of pages and result lists. In NN/g's 2018 eye-tracking, 57% of viewing time was above the fold and 74% in the first two screenfuls. In product grids, attention favors the top-left, decays more slowly than in lists, and users skip rows. Randomized ranking experiments (Expedia) show that position causally changes clicks/search but not utility conditional on a click. On Amazon, the top results attract clicks even when relevance and price are worse.

### Cited Findings
- **NN/g "Scrolling and Attention" (2018 eye-tracking, 120 participants, >130,000 fixations, 1920×1080 desktop):** **57% of viewing time above the fold** (vs. 80% in the 2010 study at 1024×768), **74% in the first two screenfuls**, 81% in the first three, 17% in the second screenful and 26% beyond two screenfuls. On general pages, >42% of viewing time was in the top 20% of the page and >65% in the top 40%. Tasks included e-commerce pages. Unstructured pages often show an F-pattern. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- **Grid layouts:** a review in Raj & Ekstrand (arXiv 2309.10271, 2023) summarizes the eye-tracking/click literature. It covers central fixation bias (Tatler 2007) and F-patterns in grid recommenders (Zhao et al., RecSys 2016). In grid image search (Xie et al., SIGIR 2017; WWW 2019), it reports **"middle bias," "slower decay"** (attention decreases more slowly than in linear lists) and **"row skipping"** (users jump past rows). Guo et al. (KDD 2020, Etsy) confirmed row skipping and slower decay in **grid-based e-commerce product search** logs and built propensity models on them. — [arXiv 2309.10271](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.10271); [Etsy summary of Guo et al. 2020](https://www.dsml.etsy.com/publication-summaries/debiasing-grid-based-product-search-in-e-commerce)
- Djamasbi, Siegel & Tullis (HCII 2011) and an ERAU Human Factors study found that users viewed the **top-left quadrants of a grid most**, with little consensus on how they read the order of grid results (secondary, via search). — [ERAU, "First Look: Examining the Horizontal Grid Layout Using Eye-Tracking"](https://portfolio.erau.edu/en/publications/first-look-examining-the-horizontal-grid-layout-using-eye-trackin/)
- **Amazon position bias:** Rock, Strauss, O'Reilly & Mazzucato (2024), *Information Economics and Policy* 69, DOI 10.1016/j.infoecopol.2024.101115. Using over 2,000 Amazon Marketplace queries, they find that position, adjusted for on-screen prominence, "strongly shapes whether a user clicks." Within the top five results, typically four of which are ads, users become less sensitive to relevance and price. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/iepoli/v69y2024ics0167624524000374.html)
- **Causal position effect:** Ursu (2018), "The Power of Rankings," *Marketing Science* 37(4): 530–552. An Expedia field experiment with randomized hotel rankings finds that rankings causally affect search (clicks). Conditional on a click, consumers derive no extra utility from buying a higher-ranked option. — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mksc.2017.1072); [INFORMS press release](https://www.informs.org/News-Room/INFORMS-Releases/News-Releases/Do-we-really-buy-those-Top-Rated-deals-when-we-shop-online-New-study-says-rankings-matter-but-how-so-may-surprise-you)
- Retailer practice on mobile PLPs (Moovweb, secondary): 54% list layout vs. 46% grid, and only about 16% let users switch. Spec-heavy categories favored lists and apparel favored grids. — [Retail Dive](https://www.retaildive.com/news/what-retailers-selling-on-mobile-can-learn-from-tinder/415003/)

### Inferences
- A realistic click heatmap from TwinStore's grid should show: a top-heavy distribution, with the first row/first screen getting most clicks; a left-column (or center) advantage; decay with row that is *slower* than a list's 1/rank-like decay; and occasional row-skipping, not strict sequential scanning. Comparing the simulated click share by (row, column) with these qualitative regularities is a micro-level face-validity test.
- A useful quantitative target for the "above the fold" share is roughly 57% of attention in the first viewport and about 74% within two (desktop, NN/g 2018). On mobile the viewport is smaller, so expect more scrolling but the same top-heavy shape.

### Gaps
- I found no public source with a numeric click-through-rate table by grid position for e-commerce (e.g., "row 1 = X% of clicks"). The often-quoted "top three Amazon results get 64% of clicks" could not be traced to a primary source.
- I did not find a primary NN/g/Baymard study with quantitative mobile-vs-desktop PLP scanning differences.

## 4. Review reading behavior

### Takeaway
Nearly all online shoppers read reviews at least sometimes, and most read a handful (1–10) before buying. The presence of even a few reviews sharply raises purchase likelihood. Purchase likelihood peaks for ratings of about 4.0–4.7 rather than 5.0. Review distributions are typically J-shaped because of self-selection.

### Cited Findings
- **Spiegel Research Center (Northwestern/Medill, with PowerReviews data, first published 2017):** "A product with five reviews has a purchase likelihood **270% higher** than one with no reviews," with rapidly diminishing gains after the first five. Displaying reviews raised conversion by **190% for lower-priced and 380% for higher-priced products**. **Purchase likelihood typically peaks at 4.0–4.7 stars and declines as ratings approach 5.0.** Verified-buyer badges raise purchase odds by 15%. Anonymous reviewers are significantly more likely to give 1–2 stars. — [Spiegel Research Center](https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/How-Online-Reviews-Influence-Sales)
- **PowerReviews:** a 2021 survey of 6,538 US consumers found that 99.9% read reviews at least sometimes when shopping online (up from 97% in 2018), 98% call reviews essential and 57% read reviews while in physical stores. The 2018 study found that 85% seek out negative reviews and that **"the majority of consumers read between one and 10 reviews before making a purchase."** These figures are secondary, via PowerReviews summaries and trade press. — [PowerReviews 2021 survey](https://powerreviews.com/insights/power-of-reviews-survey-2021/); [PowerReviews 2018](https://powerreviews.com/consumers-depend-on-reviews/)
- **BrightLocal (local businesses, US):** the 2025 survey reports consumers more forgiving about review counts and recency. Secondary coverage of the 2026 survey (n = 1,002) reports that **68% will only use a business rated ≥4 stars (55% a year earlier) and 31% require ≥4.5 (17% a year earlier)**, and that 47% avoid businesses with fewer than 20 reviews. This is local services, not e-commerce products. — [BrightLocal 2025](https://brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey-2025/); [Cloutly summary of 2026](https://cloutly.com/blog/how-many-reviews-do-you-need/)
- **J-shaped distributions:** Hu, Pavlou & Zhang (2006 ACM EC; CACM 52(10): 144–147, 2009; *MIS Quarterly* 2017, "On Self-Selection Biases in Online Product Reviews") show that most Amazon products have an asymmetric bimodal (J-shaped) rating distribution. They attribute it to **purchasing bias** (only those expecting to like a product buy and review it) and **under-reporting bias** (extreme raters are more likely to post). When all participants in an experiment had to rate the product, the distribution was approximately normal. Details are secondary; the CACM page returned 403. — [CACM 2009 listing](https://cacmb4.acm.org/magazines/2009/10/42353-overcoming-the-j-shaped-distribution-of-product-reviews); [SUFE summary of MISQ 2017](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/409215)
- MeasuringU's 2016 retail benchmark found that "unreliable reviews" were a key trust problem for Amazon and Etsy. The 2026 mass-merchant study cites fake reviews and counterfeits as Amazon's detractor theme. — [MeasuringU 2016](https://measuringu.com/retail-ux2016/); [MeasuringU 2026](https://measuringu.com/ux-nps-benchmark-report-for-mass-merchant-websites-2026/)

### Inferences
- Simulated shoppers should read reviews on most product-page visits that lead to a purchase. Reading should be shallow (a few reviews, often including negatives). A purchase-likelihood curve that rises with review count but flattens after about 5, and that peaks below a perfect 5.0, would match the Spiegel pattern.
- If TwinStore generates in-store review content, the star distribution should be J-shaped (many 5s, some 1s, few 2–3s), not normal.
- The exit-survey satisfaction scale differs from review ratings. A prompted, everyone-answers survey should be closer to unimodal and left-skewed, not J-shaped (Hu et al.'s forced-rating experiment).

### Gaps
- I found no primary e-commerce eye-tracking/clickstream study giving the *number* of reviews actually viewed per product page. The "1–10" figure is self-reported (PowerReviews 2018).

## 5. Patience and waiting (page load time)

### Takeaway
Users are highly sensitive to delay. The tolerable wait for a page is about 2 s without feedback (Nah 2004). Google's 2017–2018 model estimates that bounce probability rises 32% from 1→3 s, 90% from 1→5 s and 123% from 1→10 s. Akamai retail data show conversion falling by several percent per extra 100 ms and bounce up to 103% higher with a 2 s delay. Server response time reduces the probability of continuing a session in clickstream models.

### Cited Findings
- **Google (Daniel An, Feb 2018, "Find out how you stack up to new industry benchmarks for mobile page speed"):** "As page load time goes from one second to 10 seconds, the probability of a mobile site visitor bouncing increases **123%**." This comes from a deep neural network with 90% prediction accuracy (Google/SOASTA Research, 2017). As page elements go from 400 to 6,000, conversion probability drops 95%. The average mobile landing page took about 15 s to fully load, and 70% took >5 s for above-the-fold content. The sample was about 11M mobile ad landing pages in 213 countries (4G, Jan 2018). — [Google/Think with Google benchmark page](https://business.google.com/ca-en/think/marketing-strategies/mobile-page-speed-new-industry-benchmarks/?authuser=2)
- The intermediate values of the same Google/SOASTA curve, **1→3 s: +32%, 1→5 s: +90%, 1→6 s: +106%**, are widely quoted but absent from the current Google page text I fetched (secondary). — [websitesetup.org compilation](https://websitesetup.org/news/website-load-time-statistics/)
- **Google/DoubleClick "The Need for Mobile Speed" (Sept 2016, 10,000+ mobile web domains):** "53% of mobile site visits are likely to be abandoned if pages take longer than 3 seconds to load." Average load time was 19 s on 3G (home pages). Sites loading in 5 s saw 70% longer sessions and 35% lower bounce than those loading in 19 s. — [Google Ad Manager blog](https://blog.google/products/admanager/the-need-for-mobile-speed/); [MarketingCharts caveats](https://www.marketingcharts.com/digital-70669)
- **Akamai (formerly SOASTA) State of Online Retail Performance, Spring 2017** (about 10 billion user visits from top retailers, one month): a 100 ms delay can hurt conversion by 7%, and a 2 s delay increases bounce rates by 103% (secondary via press coverage). — [Akamai press release](https://akamai.com/ja/newsroom/press-release/akamai-releases-spring-2017-state-of-online-retail-performance-report); [Benzinga](https://www.benzinga.com/node/9315515)
- **Akamai August 2017 data (MediaPost):** going from 2.7 → 2.8 s cut conversion by **2.4% on desktop, 7.1% on smartphones and 3.8% on tablets**. Peak-conversion load times were desktop 1.8 s, tablet 1.9 s and mobile 2.7 s. The lowest bounce rates were at 0.7–1.2 s load. 53% of mobile visitors leave pages taking >3 s. Average conversion was desktop 4.1%, mobile 1.4% and tablet 2.7%. — [MediaPost, "Three Seconds and You're Out"](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/306009/three-seconds-and-youre-out.html)
- **Nah (2004), "A study on tolerable waiting time: how long are Web users willing to wait?"** *Behaviour & Information Technology* 23(3): 153–163. In an experiment, the tolerable waiting time for information retrieval was **about 2 seconds**, and the presence of feedback prolongs it. A secondary review says feedback extended the wait to about 38 s on average, which is unverified. — [SMU InK record](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/sis_research/9983)
- **Bucklin & Sismeiro:** server response time had the largest negative coefficient on continuing the session (−8.49) and also shortened page-view durations. — [slides](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/alm3/presentations/choicesymposium2001/bucklin.pdf)
- **Session timeouts used in research:** 20 minutes of inactivity (Montgomery et al. 2004) and 30 minutes (Bucklin & Sismeiro; the conventional web-analytics default). — [Montgomery et al.](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/alm3/papers/purchase%20conversion.pdf); [Bucklin & Sismeiro](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/alm3/presentations/choicesymposium2001/bucklin.pdf)

### Inferences
- TwinStore's dwell model includes load time. A validation target is that the simulated probability of leaving after a page should rise steeply once load passes about 2–3 s, especially on mobile. Mobile should also be more sensitive per 100 ms than desktop (7.1% vs. 2.4% conversion loss in Akamai data).
- The 3–18 minute "patience" budget is broadly consistent with observed sessions. About 6 pages × about 100 s per page ≈ 10 min in the 1999 Bucklin & Sismeiro data, and NN/g task times are 5.5–8 min per task (see §6). It should yield a right-skewed session-duration distribution rather than a uniform one.

### Gaps
- The original Google/SOASTA 2017 report with the full 1→3/5/6/10 s curve was not retrieved. Only the 123% endpoint is confirmed on a current Google page.
- I found no recent peer-reviewed field study (2020+) of abandonment as a function of load time in e-commerce.

## 6. Demographic differences (age, gender, device by age)

### Takeaway
Older users are slower, less successful and less satisfied on websites. In NN/g's 2013 study, users 65+ had a 55% success rate vs. 75% for ages 21–55, took 7:49 vs. 5:28 per task and gave up about twice as often. Online buying participation declines with age: in the EU in 2025, 90% of internet users aged 25–34 bought online vs. 55% of those 65–74. Smartphone purchasing is near-universal under 50 in the US but about half among 65+. Men and women buy online at equal rates in the EU. Clickstream differences by gender exist but are weakly documented.

### Cited Findings
- **NN/g senior usability (Nielsen, May 2013):** Round 2 (2013) had 31 seniors aged 65+ on 29 sites, plus 20 controls aged 21–55. Round 1 (2002) had 44 seniors on 17 sites. Results:

  | Metric | Seniors 2002 | Seniors 2013 | Ages 21–55 (2013) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Task success | 52.5% | 55.3% | 74.5% |
  | Time on task | 9:58 | 7:49 | 5:28 |
  | Errors | 4.6 | 2.4 | 1.1 |
  | Satisfaction (1–7) | 3.7 | 4.1 | 4.6 |

  Differences were significant (p < .002; satisfaction p < .05). **Seniors gave up about twice as often**, blamed themselves for problems 90% of the time (vs. 58%), used search 51% more, and 45% had trouble managing tabs/windows. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-seniors-improvements/)
- **Eurostat 2025 (EU, ICT usage survey):** 95% of people aged 16–74 used the internet, and **78% of internet users bought online**. By age, the share of internet users buying online was **16–24: 84%; 25–34: 90%; 35–44: 87%; 45–54: 80%; 55–64: 69%; 65–74: 55%**. By sex, **men 78% and women 78%**. By education, low 59%, medium 77% and high >89%. Problems encountered: 20% slower delivery than indicated, **12% website difficult to use or not working well**, 10% wrong/damaged goods, 5% final costs higher than indicated, 4% fraud, and 63% no problems. — [Eurostat Statistics Explained](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=E-commerce_statistics_for_individuals)
- **Pew Research Center (July 5–17, 2022, n = 6,034 US adults):** 76% ever buy online via smartphone, 69% via desktop/laptop and 28% via tablet. **91% of 18–49-year-olds ever buy via smartphone, vs. 69% of 50–64 and 48% of 65+.** 32% buy via smartphone at least weekly, vs. 21% via desktop/laptop. The Pew page returned 403 to the fetcher, so the figures come from search-engine extraction of the Pew page. — [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2022/11/21/for-shopping-phones-are-common-and-influencers-have-become-a-factor-especially-for-young-adults/)
- **Device share of traffic (Contentsquare 2026 benchmark, 99B sessions, 6,500+ sites):** desktop accounts for about 30% of visits but 47% of time spent, so mobile and other devices account for about 70% of visits. This is secondary (product page / search summary). — [Contentsquare benchmark](https://go.contentsquare.com/en/digital-experience-benchmark-product)
- **Gender:** Contentsquare (2014, 4,000 French participants, 20 e-commerce sites; industry, secondary) reported that women clicked about 30% more, viewed 12% more pages, hesitated less and bought about 7% faster. A 2013 ECIS paper found women needed less time and had shorter click paths in product-configuration tasks. A 2021 systematic review of 61 articles finds more gender differences than similarities: men hold more favorable attitudes to e-tailers and e-payment, and women are more affected by social influence and privacy concerns. — [RetailTouchPoints on Contentsquare](https://www.retailtouchpoints.com/news/content-square-study-investigates-behavioral-differences-on-e-commerce-sites/14658/); [BI Norwegian Business School systematic review](https://biopen.bi.no/bi-xmlui/handle/11250/3011231)
- Montgomery et al.'s B&N panel averaged 46 years old, and slightly more than half were female. Their model found demographics predicted page choice in the browsing state, but much less in the deliberation state. — [Montgomery et al. 2004](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/alm3/papers/purchase%20conversion.pdf)

### Inferences
- If TwinStore's older personas (60s+) show longer time on task (about 1.4× younger), lower completion (about 0.75× younger), more search use and lower satisfaction, that matches the direction and rough size of the NN/g data. The ratio of senior to young task time (7:49 / 5:28 ≈ 1.43) and of success rates (55.3 / 74.5 ≈ 0.74) are concrete targets.
- The ~55% mobile default is conservative relative to 2025–2026 traffic (about 70% of visits non-desktop per Contentsquare). Device should also covary with age: lower smartphone purchasing among 65+.
- Gender should *not* drive large differences in purchase incidence (EU 78% vs. 78%). Any simulated gender gap in conversion should be small. Browsing-style differences are weakly evidenced.

### Gaps
- Ofcom (UK) online-shopping-by-age figures were not retrieved.
- I found no rigorous recent study of e-commerce clickstream differences by gender with conversion outcomes.
- NN/g's newer (post-2013) senior or Gen Z studies were not retrieved.

## 7. Accessibility prevalence and behavior

### Takeaway
Visual limitations are common. About 2.2 billion people have near or distance vision impairment, about 1.8 billion had presbyopia in 2015 (826 million uncorrected), and about 8% of European-ancestry men vs. about 0.4–0.5% of women have red-green color-vision deficiency. Sites are inaccessible: shopping home pages average 71 WCAG errors (WebAIM 2026) and 83.9% of home pages have low-contrast text. Disabled shoppers click away: 69% in the UK Click-Away Pound 2019 survey.

### Cited Findings
- **WHO fact sheet (10 Feb 2026):** at least **2.2 billion** people have near or distance vision impairment, of which at least 1 billion are preventable or unaddressed, including **826 million with near vision impairment (presbyopia)**, 94M cataract and 88.4M refractive error. Most people with vision impairment are over 50. — [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/blindness-and-visual-impairment)
- **Fricke et al. (2018), *Ophthalmology*:** about **1.8 billion people had presbyopia in 2015 (prevalence 25%; 95% CI 1.7–2.0B)**, of whom 826M had near VI from no or inadequate correction. Unmet need is about 45%. — [AAO summary](https://aao.org/editors-choice/study-reveals-significant-worldwide-burden-from-un); [paper PDF](https://eyenovia.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Global-Prevalence-of-Presbyopia-and-Vision-Impairment-from-Uncorrected-Presbyopia.pdf)
- **Birch (2012), "Worldwide prevalence of red-green color deficiency,"** *JOSA A* 29(3): 313–320, DOI 10.1364/JOSAA.29.000313. From large random population surveys: **about 8% of European Caucasian men and about 0.4% of women**, and about 4–6.5% of men of Chinese and Japanese ethnicity. This is a secondary abstract rendering. Reviews state "up to 8% of males and 0.5% of females of Northern European descent." — [abstract via SynapseSocial](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W1975246823); [2025 review, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12385717/)
- **WebAIM Million 2026 (Feb 2026, top 1,000,000 home pages):** 95.9% had detected WCAG failures, averaging 56.1 errors per page. Error types: **low-contrast text 83.9%**, missing alt text 53.1%, missing form labels 51%, empty links 46.3%, empty buttons 30.6%. The **Shopping category (95,039 home pages) averaged 71.0 errors per page (26.6% above average)**. Shopify sites averaged 75.1, Magento 75.8 and PrestaShop 143.2. — [WebAIM Million](https://webaim.org/projects/million/)
- **WebAIM Screen Reader User Survey #10 (Dec 2023 – Jan 2024, n = 1,539):** 89.9% use a screen reader due to a disability. 91.3% use one on mobile, though 40.2% mainly use desktop. **71.6% navigate lengthy pages by headings first.** The most problematic items are ranked 1) CAPTCHA, 2) menus/tabs/dialogs not behaving as expected, 3) unclear links/buttons, 4) unexpected screen changes, 5) lack of keyboard access, 6) missing/improper alt text, 7) complex forms … 11) inaccessible or missing search. For tasks like shopping or banking, 58% prefer an app and 42% the website. — [WebAIM SR Survey 10](https://webaim.org/projects/screenreadersurvey10/)
- **Click-Away Pound 2019 (UK, published early 2020):** **69% of disabled online consumers "will simply click away" from sites they find difficult to use.** 83% limit their shopping to sites they know are accessible, 86% have paid more for a product from an accessible site and only 8% contact the site owner. 72% hit barriers on more than a quarter of sites they visit for the first time (73% in 2016). The estimate is 4.9M shoppers with £17.1bn spending power (£11.75bn in the 2016 edition). — [Click-Away Pound 2019 press release PDF](https://www.clickawaypound.com/downloads/CAP2019PR2.pdf); [AbilityNet](https://abilitynet.org.uk/news-blogs/research-shows-businesses-lose-17-billion-ignoring-accessibility-needs)
- NN/g 2013 capability scores (vision / dexterity / memory): seniors 82% / 73% / 49% vs. ages 21–55 95% / 95% / 63%. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-seniors-improvements/)

### Inferences
- TwinStore's eyesight variants map onto real prevalence. A red-green color-blind share of about 8% of male and about 0.5% of female personas is the population-faithful default for European-ancestry samples, lower for East Asian samples (4–6.5% of men). "Aging eyes" (presbyopia) should be the majority condition for personas aged 45+.
- A behavioral target for impaired-vision personas is a high click-away rate when barriers appear (about 69% leave), with almost no complaints sent (about 8%). Low-contrast text is the single most prevalent real barrier (83.9% of home pages), so simulated low-vision personas should frequently encounter and report it.

### Gaps
- The 2016 Click-Away Pound "71% click away" figure was not verified; the 2019 figure is 69%.
- I found no quantitative clickstream study of how color-blind or low-vision shoppers' click paths differ from others' on real e-commerce sites.

## 8. Traffic source behavior (landing page and behavior by channel)

### Takeaway
Real visitors rarely enter through the home page. In the B&N panel, 60% of sessions started on an information/product-type page and only 16% on the home page. Channel-level bounce/conversion benchmarks exist only from vendors, with inconsistent definitions. Email and referral traffic tend to convert best and social worst, but I could not tie specific numbers to a reliable primary source.

### Cited Findings
- B&N (April 2002): first page viewed per session was **information page 60%, home 16% and category 16%**, so users "do not randomly enter the site." — [Montgomery et al. 2004](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/alm3/papers/purchase%20conversion.pdf)
- Li & Kannan (2014), "Attributing Conversions in a Multichannel Online Marketing Environment," *JMR* 51(1): 40–56, DOI 10.1509/jmr.13.0050. This is a three-stage model (consideration of channels → visits via display, organic/paid search, referral, email → purchase) with carryover and spillover across channels. They show last-click attribution misallocates credit. Per-channel conversion numbers were not retrieved. — [UMD dissertation (Li 2014)](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/48f04dab-8fd0-474a-9c84-3df6b172327f/full); [MSI working paper](https://www.msi.org/working-papers/attribution-modeling-understanding-the-influence-of-channels-in-the-online-purchase-funnel)
- Contentsquare 2026: AI-referred traffic was 0.2% of traffic. — [Contentsquare](https://contentsquare.com/blog/bounce-rate-benchmarks/)

### Inferences
- TwinStore's source → landing mapping (search → product page, social → popular product, email → deals, direct → home) is consistent with the empirical fact that most sessions start deep in the site rather than at the home page. If the simulated direct/home share of entries is far above about 16–30%, that would be inconsistent with the B&N path data.

### Gaps
- I found no primary, methodologically transparent micro-level source (2020+) for bounce rate and conversion by traffic channel. Vendor figures (e.g., email about 5%, social <1%) are widely repeated but could not be attributed reliably. The other researchers' aggregate-KPI notes may cover this.

## 9. Abandonment reasons (survey-based)

### Takeaway
In Baymard's annual US survey, the single most common reason for abandoning a cart is "just browsing / not ready to buy" (42%). Among the remaining reasons, extra costs dominate (40%), followed by slow delivery (20%), distrust with card details (19%), forced account creation (18%), long or complicated checkout (17%) and site errors (17%).

### Cited Findings
- **Baymard Institute (page updated 22 Sept 2025, "latest quantitative study"):** **42% of US online shoppers have abandoned a cart because "I was just browsing / not ready to buy."** Excluding that segment, the reasons were: **extra costs too high (shipping, tax, fees) 40%; delivery too slow 20%; didn't trust site with card information 19%; site wanted me to create an account 18%; too long/complicated checkout 17%; website had errors/crashed 17%; returns policy not satisfactory 13%; couldn't see/calculate total cost up-front 12%; credit card declined 10%; not enough payment methods 9%; don't know 7%.** The documented average cart abandonment rate is 70.22% across 50 studies. — [Baymard cart-abandonment list](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate)
- Sample and question (secondary, Statista): the 2025 wave had **1,026 US adult respondents** answering "Have you abandoned any online purchases during the checkout process in the past 3 months? If so, for what reasons?" The survey has been repeated annually since 2017. The 2022 wave had 4,384 responses. — [Statista](https://statista.com/statistics/1228452/reasons-for-abandonments-during-checkout-united-states)
- Eurostat 2025 problems actually experienced by EU online buyers: slower delivery 20%, website difficult to use or not working 12%, final costs higher than indicated 5%. — [Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=E-commerce_statistics_for_individuals)
- MeasuringU 2026 top verbatim problems on mass-merchant sites: clutter, out-of-stock items, item quality and third-party sellers. The text gives no percentages. — [MeasuringU 2026](https://measuringu.com/ux-nps-benchmark-report-for-mass-merchant-websites-2026/)

### Inferences
- The "biggest frustration" field in TwinStore's exit survey can be compared, for shoppers who added to cart but didn't buy, against Baymard's ranked list. Costs/shipping should top the list, then delivery speed, trust, forced account and checkout complexity. About 4 in 10 non-buyers should say they were just browsing, which aligns with the browse mission share.

### Gaps
- Baymard's device split of abandonment reasons (mobile vs. desktop) was not available on the public page.

## 10. UX/survey benchmarks for the exit survey (SEQ, SUS, NPS, ACSI, CSAT, distribution shape)

### Takeaway
Typical task-level SEQ averages about 5.3–5.6 on the 7-point scale, above the midpoint of 4. The average SUS is 68. *Website-experience* NPS for mass merchants is low (about +6 to +10 on average, Amazon +21 to +67, Walmart about −30). Brand-level retail NPS is higher (about 30–55). ACSI scores online retailers around 79–85 on a 0–100 scale. Usability-test satisfaction on a 1–7 scale averaged 4.1 (seniors) to 4.6 (ages 21–55) in NN/g's 2013 study.

### Cited Findings
- **SEQ (MeasuringU, Sauro, 2012; average updated May 2019):** across "over 400 tasks and 10,000 users," the average "hovers between about **5.3 and 5.6**" on the 7-point scale (midpoint 4). It correlates about r = .5 with task time and completion. **About 14% of users who failed a task still rated it "extremely easy."** Some users rate everything 6–7. — [MeasuringU SEQ](https://measuringu.com/seq10/)
- **SUS (MeasuringU, 2018):** the average is **68** (50th percentile). 75 is the 73rd percentile and 52 the 15th. The curved grade scale runs A+ ≥ 84.1, A 80.8–84.0, B 74.1–77.1, C 65.0–71.0, D 51.7–62.6 and F < 51.7. Promoters average a SUS of about 81, passives about 70 and detractors ≤53. SUS explains 30–50% of the variation in likelihood to recommend. — [MeasuringU, 5 Ways to Interpret a SUS Score](https://measuringu.com/interpret-sus-score/)
- **Retail website NPS and SUPR-Q (MeasuringU 2016, n = 935, 10 sites):** the average likelihood-to-recommend NPS was **10%**, with **Amazon 67%, Etsy 44% and Walmart −35%**. The average SUPR-Q was the 78th percentile, with Amazon at the 99th (SUS-equivalent usability 85). — [MeasuringU retail 2016](https://measuringu.com/retail-ux2016/)
- **Mass-merchant website NPS (MeasuringU 2026; fieldwork Nov–Dec 2025, n = 351 US users, 7 sites):** **TJ Maxx +25, Amazon +21 and Walmart −30, with an average of +6.** SUPR-Q averaged the 54th percentile, ranging from the 75th (TJ Maxx) to the 12th (Walmart). All five sites measured in both 2021 and 2025 declined significantly. Key LTR drivers were easily finding deals, clear product images and inspiration. — [MeasuringU 2026](https://measuringu.com/ux-nps-benchmark-report-for-mass-merchant-websites-2026/)
- **Brand-level retail NPS (vendor, secondary):** QuestionPro Q1 2025 (1,000 US consumers) gives retail about 37. Comparably lists Amazon at 49 (66% promoters / 17% passives / 17% detractors) and Costco at 50 (68 / 14 / 18). Verint 2022 ranked Costco first at 55.3. — [QuestionPro](https://www.questionpro.com/blog/nps-in-retail/); [Comparably Amazon](https://www.comparably.com/brands/amazon); [Comparably Costco](https://www.comparably.com/brands/costco); [CX Today on Verint](https://www.cxtoday.com/?p=45206)
- **ACSI online retail (Retail & Consumer Shipping Study, released Jan 2025, 41,850 surveys; 0–100):** Chewy 85 (leader all three years measured), **Amazon 83**, eBay 81, and the industry average 79 (secondary press coverage; theacsi.com returned 403). An earlier year had Costco 83 over Amazon 82. — [MacTech on ACSI 2025](https://www.mactech.com/2025/01/28/apple-store-satisfaction-drops-5-in-latest-acsi-retail-and-consumer-shopping-study/amp/); [MarTech Cube on ACSI](https://www.martechcube.com/?p=243401); [Fox Business (earlier year)](https://foxbusiness.com/retail/costco-dethrones-amazon-as-internet-retail-king-report-says)
- **Satisfaction in usability tests (1–7):** NN/g 2013 found seniors 4.1 and ages 21–55 4.6 (2002 seniors: 3.7). — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-seniors-improvements/)
- **Distribution shape:** voluntary online reviews are J-shaped (Hu, Pavlou & Zhang), but forced ratings by all participants were approximately normal. SEQ responses skew high, with a mean of about 5.5 against a midpoint of 4 and some respondents always answering 6–7. — [CACM 2009](https://cacmb4.acm.org/magazines/2009/10/42353-overcoming-the-j-shaped-distribution-of-product-reviews); [MeasuringU SEQ](https://measuringu.com/seq10/)

### Inferences
- Validation targets for TwinStore's exit survey are as follows. A mean SEQ of about 5.3–5.6 for typical tasks, and lower for failing/abandoning shoppers. Satisfaction (1–7) around 4–5, lower for older or impaired personas. Website NPS near single digits to the low 20s for an average mass-merchant-like store, not brand-level 30–50. NPS should correlate with ease (SUS/SEQ) at r ≈ .5–.65.
- A simulated satisfaction or NPS distribution that is J-shaped (pile-up at 7/10 plus a spike at 1/0) would look like *voluntary* reviews, not a prompted exit survey. A unimodal, left-skewed distribution is the more realistic target.
- The 14% "failed but rated it very easy" anomaly is a useful realism check: simulated agents should not be perfectly consistent between outcome and rating.

### Gaps
- I found no e-commerce-specific CSAT benchmark from a primary source (ACSI is the closest).
- The 2026 ACSI online retail scores could not be verified on theacsi.com (HTTP 403).
- No public SEQ distribution histogram was found for e-commerce tasks specifically.

## 11. Returning visitors, multi-session journeys, visits before purchase and time to purchase

### Takeaway
Purchases usually follow multiple visits. In the 1998 Amazon panel there were 2.58 visits per visitor and each non-purchase visit raised later purchase probability with diminishing returns. In a 1999 auto site there were about 1.4 sessions per visitor. Practitioner sources put first purchase at about 3–4 sessions and 3–4 weeks after first visit, but those figures are weakly sourced. Contentsquare reports that only 13% of visitors return within 30 days.

### Cited Findings
- Amazon 1998 (Media Metrix): 2.58 visits per visitor over 8 months, 1.85 purchases per buyer and 19.4% of visitors ever bought. Visit effects accumulate but each successive visit adds less. Purchase thresholds rise after a purchase. — [Moe & Fader 2004](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/Moe-Fader-Conversion-Mgmt-Sci-2004.pdf)
- B&N 2002: most users had only one or two sessions with B&N activity, and fewer than 25% had more than two. — [Montgomery et al. 2004](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/alm3/papers/purchase%20conversion.pdf)
- Auto site 1999: 1.447 sessions per visitor in a month, and repeat visits reduced pages viewed per visit, which the authors read as "learning." — [Bucklin & Sismeiro slides](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/alm3/presentations/choicesymposium2001/bucklin.pdf)
- **Contentsquare 2026:** 13% of visitors come back within 30 days of their first visit. — [Contentsquare](https://contentsquare.com/blog/bounce-rate-benchmarks/)
- Practitioner figures (secondary, weak): first-time buyers averaged about **25 days and 3.5 sessions** from first visit to purchase (Smart Insights; undated, source data unclear). Rakuten MediaForge (2014) found about 10 days from ad exposure to purchase. Amazon shoppers made 3 searches and viewed 6 product pages before purchase (Semrush 2023). — [Smart Insights](https://www.smartinsights.com/customer-engagement/customer-engagement-strategy/latency-and-loyalty-in-retail-ecommerce/); [Mobile Marketing Magazine on Rakuten](https://mobilemarketingmagazine.com/average-consumer-considers-purchases-for-10-days-before-buying/); [Semrush Amazon funnel 2023](https://semrush.com/blog/amazon-funnel-study-2023)
- Metric definitions ("Visits to Purchase", "Days to Purchase") follow Kaushik. — [Kaushik, Occam's Razor](https://www.kaushik.net/avinash/?p=79)

### Inferences
- TwinStore simulates single visits with a "would-return" survey item. The evidence suggests real purchase journeys often span multiple sessions, so a single-visit simulation will understate eventual conversion for "compare", "gift" and "search/deliberation" missions. The paper should frame simulated per-visit conversion as per-visit, not per-shopper. "Would return" answers can be sanity-checked against the low observed 30-day return rate (13%). Stated intentions typically exceed actual return behavior.

### Gaps
- I found no recent peer-reviewed estimate of the distribution of sessions-to-first-purchase across e-commerce categories. The 3.5-session / 25-day figure is a single practitioner source.
